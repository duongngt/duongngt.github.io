Sunrise is the most beautiful moment of the day; it fills us with a sense of hope. In Da Nang, all you need is to wake up a little early to catch it. For me, mornings at Non Nuoc beach are not only about watching the sun — they are a way to put my thoughts in order before a day of work.

## 5 a.m. at Non Nuoc

While most of the city is still asleep, the beach is already alive. The sky is still deep blue and the streetlights along the coastal road haven't switched off, yet the sand is full of people. Groups of older aunties and uncles do their morning exercises, stretching their arms in a shared rhythm. A few young people jog along the water's edge, leaving footprints the waves erase a moment later. Someone swims quietly far out, just a head bobbing between the waves.

The air at that hour is cool and slightly salty. The sand is still cold underfoot, damp after the night. The sea breeze carries the smell of seaweed, salt and a hint of fish from the boats that have just come in.

## Basket boats and the people of the sea

One of my favorite sights is fishermen pulling their basket boats ashore. The round boats, woven from bamboo, are so small that from a distance they look like leaves floating on the water. A man paddles with a single oar, twisting it gently, skillfully riding over the last waves before letting the boat slide up onto the sand.

Right away, a small group — usually older women and young mothers — gathers around. Fish, squid and crabs are tipped into baskets, buyers and sellers bargain quickly, and the air fills with talk and laughter. This makeshift fish market only lasts a short while and disappears once the sun climbs higher. Arrive a little late and you will only see the boats lying on the sand, as if nothing had happened.

> The morning sea is not a still painting — it is a small neighborhood waking up, with its own rhythm set by people who have spent their whole lives with the waves.

## The moment the sun rises

Before the sun appears, the sky keeps changing color: from deep blue to purple, then pink, then orange. Sometimes a few thin clouds near the horizon catch the light first, glowing as if outlined. Then a small red dot lifts above the sea, grows, and within a few minutes becomes a blazing sphere.

That is when the whole beach seems to slow down for a beat. Joggers stop, the aunties and uncles turn to face east, and phones go up in the air. The water reflects a long strip of light stretching straight to the shore, and everything — basket boats, silhouettes, waves — is edged with warm gold.

## Better sunrise photos

I am not a professional photographer, but after many mornings, I have picked up a few lessons:

- Arrive about 20 minutes before sunrise — the best colors often appear before the sun comes up.
- Place the horizon on the lower third of the frame, and keep it level.
- Shoot against the light for beautiful silhouettes of people and basket boats.
- Find a foreground: a basket boat, footprints in the sand, someone walking — to give the photo depth.
- Lower the exposure slightly to keep the colors of the sky and avoid blown highlights.
- Don't forget to watch with your eyes, not only through the lens.

### If you are using a phone

Tap on the bright part of the sky to set focus and exposure, then drag the exposure slider down a little. Avoid digital zoom, since the image breaks up easily; walk closer to your subject instead. And wipe your lens — sea salt in the air can easily make photos hazy.

## Planning a morning at Non Nuoc

If you want to experience it yourself, here is what I usually do:

1. Check the sunrise time the night before. In summer the sun rises quite early in Da Nang; in winter, a little later.
2. Set your alarm earlier than you think you need, so you have time to get there and find a spot.
3. Bring a light jacket in the cooler months, and a bottle of water.
4. Once there, walk along the beach a bit to find an angle with basket boats or fewer people.
5. After sunrise, stop at a roadside stall for breakfast — a bowl of mi Quang or a cup of coffee is the perfect ending.

As for the time of year, the dry months — roughly from spring through the end of summer — usually bring clearer skies and calmer seas. The rainy season still has lovely sunrises, but they are much harder to predict.

## A new day

Every sunrise is a reminder: whatever yesterday was, today is still a fresh page. At work, I often have days when everything feels tangled — a bug that is hard to track down, a design that has to be redone from scratch. Mornings at the beach have taught me that nothing has to be solved all at once. The sun rises little by little, and the sky changes color gradually.

I like to think of each day as a new design: a blank canvas, some light, and the right to start over. All it takes is waking up a little early, walking to the sea, and letting yourself see it.
