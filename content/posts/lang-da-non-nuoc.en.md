Every year on the 16th day of the third lunar month, Non Nuoc stone carving village celebrates its founders' day. It is a rare moment when the sound of chisels fades, making room for processions, the scent of incense and stories about the craft told over cups of tea. I grew up in Da Nang and have passed the village more times than I can count, but it took a founders' day morning for me to truly stop, look and listen.

## Centuries of craft at the foot of the Marble Mountains

Non Nuoc village sits right at the foot of the Marble Mountains (Ngu Hanh Son), not far from the city center, on the road toward Hoi An. Stone carving here has a history of several centuries, handed down from grandfather to father, and then to son. In the old days, carvers took stone straight from the mountains; today quarrying at the Marble Mountains is prohibited to protect the landmark, so most of the stone is brought in from other regions.

Walking along the main road, you pass one workshop after another. Some display statues several times taller than a person; others are just a small yard with a few stone benches and an old carver bent over his chisel. The first thing you notice is not what you see but what you hear:

- the steady tap of hammer on chisel, regular as a ticking clock;
- the whine of a grinder rising and falling;
- water splashing over stone to wash away the dust;
- and somewhere, a radio and the chatter and laughter of young carvers.

White stone dust settles on everything, from tin roofs and motorbikes parked by the door to the carvers' hair. Stand around long enough and your own shirt turns a pale grey.

## The village's founders' day

Founders' day is when the whole village honors those who gave birth to the craft. From early morning, villagers gather at the ancestral hall, the altar is carefully arranged and incense smoke drifts through the air. There is a procession and a solemn ceremony, followed by stories: about the forefathers, about works that have traveled far, about the ups and downs the village has lived through.

What moved me most was seeing the carvers — usually covered in stone dust — dressed that day in ao dai and neatly pressed shirts, standing respectfully before the altar. Children ran around the courtyard, one of them curiously touching an unfinished statue. Perhaps a few of them will pick up a chisel one day, like their fathers and grandfathers.

> "Stone cannot speak, but a carver has to know how to listen to it." — a line I heard that morning and have never forgotten.

## From stone to sculpture

A large statue can take months, sometimes longer. One carver walked me through the process, which roughly goes like this:

1. **Choosing the stone.** The carver studies the color and veining and looks for hidden cracks. A beautiful block with an internal fracture can ruin an entire piece.
2. **Sketching.** The shape is drawn directly onto the stone, sometimes from a model, sometimes entirely from memory and experience.
3. **Rough carving.** Large chunks of excess stone are removed to reveal the overall form. This is the most physically demanding step.
4. **Detailing.** The folds of a robe, the fingers, the eyes — the things that decide whether a piece has a "soul". This step requires great skill and total focus.
5. **Grinding and polishing.** The surface is ground from coarse to fine until the stone gleams in the sunlight.

Every step demands patience and hands that know the stone. A wrong strike at the final stage may be impossible to undo, so carvers tend to move slowly, look carefully, and only then strike.

### What the village makes

From raw blocks of stone, artisans create Buddha and Guanyin statues, sacred animals such as lions, dragons and unicorns, as well as small, charming items like bracelets, figurines, bowls, plates and decorative lamps. Many works travel across the country and abroad, carrying a piece of Non Nuoc with them.

## Visiting the village: a few tips

If you would like to visit, here is what I have learned over many trips:

- **Timing:** early morning or late afternoon is the most pleasant, avoiding the harsh midday sun. To see the village in a festive mood, plan around founders' day on the 16th of the third lunar month.
- **Combine your itinerary:** the village is right below the Marble Mountains, so you can climb the mountains and visit the pagodas and caves in the morning, then walk down to the village in the afternoon.
- **Be polite with your camera:** the carvers are working, so ask before taking close-ups and don't stand too close when they are using machines.
- **Shopping:** if you want to buy something, take a stroll, compare a few workshops and don't hesitate to ask about the type and origin of the stone. Large pieces can usually be shipped.
- **What to wear:** comfortable clothes and closed-toe shoes, and be ready to collect a little stone dust.

## What I learned

Design is a lot like stone carving: you remove what is unnecessary to reveal the beauty at the core. When building an interface, I often start with many ideas and many components. But the best product is rarely the one with the most things — it is the one refined to the point where every remaining detail has a reason to be there.

The carvers also taught me about patience. They never rush to polish while the form is still wrong. In my work, I have learned to get the structure right first, and only then move on to the details and the final "polish". And just like that last strike of the chisel, some decisions deserve careful thought, because undoing them costs far more.

Every time I pass the village and hear the steady tapping of hammers drifting out of the workshops, I am reminded of that lesson: beauty does not come from adding more, but from knowing exactly what to take away.
